# Humanizer: all 33 maintained checks

Run inside G25, not as another installed skill. These are editorial patterns, **not an AI-authorship detector**. Natural prose can contain them; judge function and clusters rather than flattening voice. Keep true specifics, real asides, uneven but intelligible rhythm and the author's deliberate style. Neutral technical/reference material does not need invented personality. Do not add an anecdote, result, opinion attributed to the user, quote or factual detail merely to sound human.

This included 33-pattern roster is maintained for this writing workflow. It is not a claim that the latest upstream Humanizer has the same pattern count. Conceptual lineage: the MIT-licensed [Humanizer project](https://github.com/blader/humanizer); its attribution/license notice is included in the [skill's NOTICE](../NOTICE.md). This reference uses fresh domain-neutral descriptions and examples rather than importing a creator's private samples. Do not fetch or install another version to replace this required roster during a task.

## Process and precedence

1. Read the actual draft and any authorized voice samples. Notice sentence lengths, word choices, rhythm, punctuation, recurring phrases and transitions. Match those habits instead of regularizing every sentence.
2. Audit every numbered pattern below: PASS with evidence of a clean actual scan, REVISE with exact affected text, BLOCKED if needed evidence is unavailable, or N/A with a real reason. Formatting-only patterns can be N/A in pure spoken text; absence of a checked applicable pattern is PASS, not a fabricated hit.
3. Draft a natural edit. Preserve information, not arbitrary paragraph shape. Compress dull passages and dwell on useful proof while keeping every protected fact/condition and locked phrase.
4. Ask internally: “What still sounds artificially composed?” and “Did this add, remove or change any factual meaning, name, number, date, source, promise or protected wording?” Correct both.
5. Produce the final copy, compare it to the truth sheet and locks, run mechanical checks, then rehearse aloud. Record human/synthetic/not-run honestly. Do not print multiple humanizer drafts inside the spoken script; keep the final copy and audit below it.

Audit mode reports findings without an edit. Editing files preserves code, data, frontmatter and link targets; script version history is not deleted. A verified strong hook is not flattened into neutral prose merely because it has emotion. Deliberate repetition can be a working engine when every rung adds useful value and sounds natural. Default no em/en-dash connectors in narration; an explicit supplied voice sample may retain them. The G11 no-three-item-recital standard is stricter than pattern 10's “overuse” framing and takes precedence. The included checker does not offer a recital bypass; exact locked copy can retain a finding without being rewritten or declared passed.

## Content patterns

### 1. Inflated significance, legacy and broad trends
Flag ordinary facts dressed as pivotal changes, symbols or sweeping movements without evidence. Replace the claimed significance with the actual consequence. “This booking page marks a new era of customer connection” becomes “Customers can book without calling.” Do not exaggerate the historical meaning of a small feature.

### 2. Inflated notability and media coverage
Flag repeated prestige claims, follower tallies or a roll call of outlets that adds no relevant proof. Keep the one credential/source that explains why the viewer should trust this claim; verify it. Public attention is not expertise, endorsement or a product test.

### 3. Superficial analysis through trailing -ing phrases
Flag clauses like “highlighting its importance”, “showcasing commitment” or “reflecting a broader trend” when they append fake depth. State a real action and result, or cut the tail. Ordinary useful participles are not automatically wrong.

### 4. Generic promotional language
Replace “revolutionary”, “breathtaking”, “game-changing”, “boasts” and similar unsupported praise with a specific advantage or proof. Marketing can be enthusiastic; neutralizing all desire is not the goal. “A game-changing comparison” becomes “You can compare the same ownership costs before choosing.” Scope to evidence.

### 5. Vague attribution and weasel words
Flag “experts say”, “industry reports” and “many believe” without a real identifiable source. Name the verified source where useful or cut/hold the assertion. Never invent an expert to rescue the sentence.

### 6. Formulaic challenges/future-prospects detours
Flag obligatory “Despite these challenges, the future is bright” sections and paragraphs that mechanically rehearse pros/cons rather than advancing the story. Keep material limitations necessary for accurate understanding; move nonessential implementation detail to a resource, not hidden qualifiers to a misleading promise.

## Language and grammar patterns

### 7. Overused AI-coded vocabulary
Review clusters of “delve”, “tapestry”, “pivotal”, “landscape”, “underscore”, “foster”, “intricate” or “showcase”. Prefer the audience's ordinary verbs and concrete objects. One useful transition or formal word is not proof of AI writing. Preserve proper names and quotations.

### 8. Avoiding simple is/are/has constructions
Flag “serves as”, “stands as” or “boasts” where “is” or “has” says the same thing. “The sheet serves as a central hub” becomes “The sheet keeps the quotes together.” Keep active verbs that actually describe action.

### 9. Negative parallelism and tailing negations
Flag repeated “not just X, but Y”, “it's not X, it's Y”, and clipped tails like “no guessing” used as canned drama. State the actual change as a complete thought. Preserve a real comparison or necessary negative condition; do not remove a safety or eligibility fact.

### 10. Forced rule-of-three
Flag bundles chosen for rhythm rather than meaning. Default narration rejects any three-item recital inside a sentence under G11, even if it can be called “load-bearing”. Rewrite with an accurate umbrella term that keeps the whole verified value. “Checks the invoice, the dates and the terms” might become “Checks the agreement before you approve it” only if that accurately describes the coverage. Do not broaden three supported checks into “checks everything”. Distinct escalating sentences are not automatically a recital.

### 11. Elegant variation and synonym cycling
Flag changing a stable noun into several fancy substitutes just to avoid repetition. Keep the same clear name for the same object; change the subject only when the meaning changes. Synonyms can otherwise look like different products/actors.

### 12. False ranges
Flag “from X to Y” where the endpoints do not belong to a meaningful scale or bounded scope. Replace with the actual relationship. Real price, age, distance and time ranges remain when sourced and intelligible.

### 13. Passive voice and subjectless fragments
Identify the actor where it clarifies what happens. Merge runs such as “No forms needed. Results delivered automatically.” into ordinary connected speech. Passive voice can be right when the actor is irrelevant; active voice is not a universal quota.

## Style patterns

### 14. Em/en dashes as universal connectors
Default remove em/en dashes and spaced double hyphens from narration, using periods or real spoken connectors. Do not replace each with another fragmented slogan. An explicit voice sample can justify retaining its dash style; punctuation itself is not proof of AI authorship. Preserve protected quotations in audit mode.

### 15. Mechanical boldface
Flag indiscriminate emphasis, especially each feature being bolded. Formatting belongs outside narration; review tables can legitimately use readable labels. Humanizing must not destroy useful document structure.

### 16. Inline-header vertical lists
Flag “Performance: ... Efficiency: ... Quality: ...” as spoken bullet prose. Turn it into a causal story with ordinary connectors. Structured evidence tables and production notes are exempt; they are meant to be scanned.

### 17. Title case headings
Prefer sentence-case prose headings unless the user's document convention says otherwise. Not applicable to words spoken aloud; do not rewrite a protected product name or title.

### 18. Decorative emojis
Flag decorative emoji that substitutes for meaning or a staged marketing tone. Keep only explicit brand/format choices; do not include emoji as if a speaker should say it. This is not a claim that human writers never use emoji.

### 19. Mechanical curly quotation marks
Default clean typographic quote decoration in raw narration; quotes can live in source/production notes. A curly apostrophe inside a contraction is not an extraneous quotation mark. Automatic typography alone is not evidence of AI writing and an explicit style sample may retain it.

## Communication patterns

### 20. Chat-assistant artifacts
Remove “Certainly!”, “Here is your script”, “I hope this helps”, “Let me know” and model offers accidentally pasted into narration. A user-selected real CTA is not automatically a chat artifact; judge what the speaker is actually asking the audience to do.

### 21. Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and speculative gap filling
Do not narrate model training limitations as content or invent a plausible bio/background to fill missing research. Record an actual evidence gap in the review. A real checked/as-of date in a truth sheet remains essential, not a tell to remove.

### 22. Sycophantic or servile tone
Cut fake agreement, reflexive praise and “you're absolutely right” as filler. Respectful language remains. Do not add a stance the author never took.

## Filler and hedging

### 23. Filler phrases
Replace “in order to” with “to”, “due to the fact that” with “because”, and other padded setup with direct speech. Cutting must preserve causal relationships and scope. Brevity is useful when meaning stays intact.

### 24. Excessive hedging
Remove stacked “could potentially possibly” hedges. Keep real uncertainty, probabilistic claims and limitations supported by evidence. Confidence cannot convert an inference into an observed result.

### 25. Generic positive conclusions
Cut “exciting times ahead”, “the future looks bright” and ceremonial wrap-ups with no new value. End on strongest earned proof/payoff and specific next action instead. A CTA should grow from the value, not become a tax on the viewer.

### 26. Uniform hyphenated word pairs
Flag mechanically hyphenating every compound in every grammatical position. Prefer natural readable syntax, keeping hyphens where needed for meaning or conventional terminology. This mainly affects prose, not a speaker's sound; do not spend narration fixing typography.

### 27. Persuasive authority tropes
Flag “the real question”, “at its core”, “the heart of the matter”, “what really matters” when they manufacture profundity before an ordinary claim. State the useful question/fact directly. A genuinely important distinction needs concrete proof, not ceremony.

### 28. Announcements and signposting
Cut “let's dive in”, “without further ado”, “here's what you need to know” and tutorial run-ups that add no value. Start doing/explaining the useful thing. A necessary orientation in a longer instructional format can remain when it earns its time.

### 29. Fragmented headings and restatement warm-ups
Flag a heading followed by a one-line restatement before the actual point. In narration, remove the equivalent empty warm-up. Evidence headings can stay; do not force the audit into a speech.

### 30. Diff-anchored writing
Flag current descriptions that only narrate what changed from a previous draft. Make the current script understandable without old versions. Revision notes and changelogs are correctly version-scoped and should retain real change reasons.

### 31. Manufactured punchlines and staccato drama
Flag repeated fragments and a quotable closer on every sentence. One short emphasis can work; a whole stack sounds read from slogans. Merge into connected speech without losing the earned emotional build. No automatic destruction of a source-supported growing roster.

### 32. Aphorism formulas
Flag “X is the Y of Z”, “the currency of”, “the architecture of” or “not a tool but a mirror” when they sound profound without precision. Replace with the actual mechanism or consequence. A clear relevant analogy can stay when it aids understanding and does not make a false factual comparison.

### 33. Fake-candid rhetorical openers
Flag standalone “Honestly?”, “Look”, “Here's the thing”, “Let's be honest” and “Real talk” used to stage intimacy before an ordinary point. Say the point. The same words occurring naturally mid-sentence are not automatically a failure.

## False positives and final factual parity

Professional grammar, formal vocabulary, a single dash, ordinary transitions, curly typography, a single short sentence, deliberate repetition and proper names are not reliable AI indicators alone. Review clusters and function; do not label someone's authorship from style. Match a real voice sample without inventing personality, personal history or private details.

Compare pre/post claim meaning, actors, quantities/units, dates, conditions, eligibility, links, attribution, resource promise, disclosures and locked wording. Every added factual detail needs a source and a new truth-sheet entry; otherwise remove it. Every lost qualification needs restoration or a truthfully narrower claim. Then rerun all gates, not only G25.
